Earlier in the month I attended a smaller security conference called [0xcon](https://0xcon.co.za/){:target="_blank"}, that’s held in Johannesburg every year. Full disclosure, one of my friends is an organizer of this conference, and he’s done a stellar job of organizing it for the last nine years. The aim of the conference is to provide a platform for first time speakers, a place where people can just share cool things they’re doing, while being non-aligned to any specific security company (read: little to no sponsors).

## My first 0xcon

I remember how excited I was the first year I was in infosec. I wanted the 0xcon conference to have workshops, and hackathons and talks and food and everything! In my head, I wanted what I thought a mini-Defcon would look like, even though I had never been to Defcon before. I asked my friend if there could be a workshop, specifically on lockpicking. He just said, “Yes, you should run with that”, and went about his business.

Huh? I wanted to ATTEND a lockpicking workshop, not host one. Either way, after a lot of asking around, I finally found a guy that would host it with me. This meant I had to learn lockpicking, create some sort of presentation, find lockpicking sets (our company sponsored those, thanks SP!), coordinate with my co-host, and get everything ready for the conference. But, I had one tiny issue. I am deathly afraid of speaking in public. (This is, unfortunately, not the story of a hero’s journey, my public speaking part was a disaster.)

In the ramp up to the conference, we had people book tickets for the lockpicking workshop, so I was mentally prepared for about 15-20 people in the room when I presented. What happened instead, was that the lockpicking room ended up being the place where everyone gathered before the talks, and I had about 50+ people staring at me, as I fumbled my way through the history of locks and lockpicking. I also may or may not have hyperventilated. I can’t remember. Were it not for my co-host, the whole workshop would have been a disaster. (Higgo, you legend!)

### Lessons learned

Even though my first security conference ended up being a traumatic event, and I missed all of the talks, quite a few lessons were learned.
1.	<s>I should not speak in public.</s> I should probably just work on this (but I probably won’t, the fear is very real).
2.	Small conferences don’t have to be what the bigger conferences are.
3.	You can’t watch talks AND participate in workshops at the same time, which sucks, because I want to do it all.
4.	Some organizational lessons go in here (herding people into the right places, etc).
5.	Something about not giving busy people more things to do, because you end up having to do it yourself anyway.
6.	The importance of doing life with cool people who have your back.

### 0xcon is (still) cool
Even though my first experience at 0xcon was not nearly as much fun as it should have been, I still enjoy going to the conference. This may be an infosec thing in general, but I love being surrounded by people that are so much smarter than me. And people in infosec are supersmart. But also humble, and maybe not aware of how smart they are.

I also LOVE learning new things, and the variety of different talks is always a winner. 

With that being said, here’s what happened at 0xcon this year (2025).

## Highlights of 0xcon 2025

We started out with a keynote by Fabian Yamaguchi, who entertained us with his story of how he got into hacking. Loved this talk, as we went on the journey with Fabs, from Germany to Black Hat and all the way to South Africa, where he now heads up Whirly Labs. Key takeaway from this talk was that if you have a vision, no matter how vague at the start, you can make it happen. (Even become or create a zero-day machine).

Next, we had Jason Deverell, who talked about strategies for the Secure Development Life Cycle of software, in order to reduce persistent vulnerabilities. An interesting outlook on how developers keep making the same mistakes, due to weak feedback loops, deadline pressures, and lack of individual accountability on projects.

The most thought-provoking talk of the day (for me), was by Jared Naude, where he talked about Chat Control in the EU. He explained what Chat Control is, how it works, what the impact would be on end-to-end encryption and privacy, and the ethical dangers surrounding it. The EU is touting child protection as the main reason for bringing in Chat Control, but one has to wonder if this is all just a ploy to enact mass surveillance? (It probably is). Makes you think, though, because we would all like a world where no one can abuse children, but would we agree to that if our own privacy was at stake? What if we didn’t agree with the government/EU, and we became the dissidents? Would you still agree to Chat Control? Like I said, thought-provoking.

Next was a talk with a ‘lekker’ vibe. Renico Koen showed us some research done on security issues in Vibe-coded applications. He also had good-looking charts showing the number of errors in code written by different LLMs. In the research, they tried various prompts on the LLMs, going from minimal security instructions to more robust security instructions, and noting how the error-rate of the different LLMs changed. 

![Slides from 0xcon talks](/assets/images/0xcon_slides.png "Slides from 0xcon talks")

After that, we had a very passionate Johan Taute, who dreams of hacking mobile networks. He gave us a rundown of the history of radio, and how that shaped today’s attack surface. Keep watching this space, this guy is determined.

Ivan Burke then gave us a look at the impact of [SANReN’s](https://www.csc.ac.za/){:target="_blank"} yearly cybersecurity competitions. After hosting this competition for a few years now, they’ve collected valuable data on the competitors that participated. The most interesting part of this talk was the data related to how many people ended up in cybersecurity after these competitions, and how many of them actually stayed in the information security field.

Ever wondered how to hack LTE modems? Rogan Dawes gave us the lowdown on what these modems look like (on the inside, obvs), and how to go about hacking them. Hardware is not my strong suit, but Rogan is extremely smart and has been hacking since, well, forever. A great talk for anyone wondering how embedded devices work, and how to approach the hacking of these devices.

Next up, my favourite hacking talk of the day, was Mohammad Pathan showing us how to hack GraphQL. He spoke about the differences between REST APIs and GraphQL, explained some of the GraphQL vulnerabilities, and then did a fun demo on an application that he built himself for practicing GraphQL vulnerabilities. Informative and practical. (More of this, please!)

I unfortunately missed the last two talks, but hope to catch them on YouTube as soon as they are uploaded. These two talks were ‘Threat modelling in an API test’ by Phalo-Mihlali Mbangcolo, and ‘Trust but verify: Using LLMs to catch what scanners miss’ by Luis de Sousa.
