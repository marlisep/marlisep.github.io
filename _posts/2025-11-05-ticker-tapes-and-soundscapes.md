Do you think in images or in sounds? Do you have an internal voice, or is it just quiet in your mind?
I recently listened to this [podcast](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/enhance-learning-speed-neuroscience-poppy-crum){:target="_blank"} between Dr. Andrew Huberman and Dr. Poppy Crum, and it was so good, I listened to it twice. 
The thing I couldn’t stop thinking about, was Dr. Crum’s way of perceiving the world.  She said, and I’m paraphrasing a bit, that she hears sounds and immediately her brain goes, “that’s a G”, “that’s an E”. Absolute pitch. Knowing what note a sound is making, and also what frequency the sound is. 

Mind blowing.

I mean, I’ve heard of terms like synesthesia (a blending of the senses, where people sometimes taste colours and see sounds) and aphantasia (being unable to create visual images), but I never really thought about it too deeply. 

I never really thought about the way that I perceive the world either, but after speaking to a few people, it seems like it’s not the norm.

## A world in subtitles
I think in words, with auditory perception as my second “brain language”. When I hear something, my brain shows me the actual words. As I typed this, the words also showed up in my mind’s eye. When I listen to songs, and focus on the words, it looks a lot like the Spotify lyrics, moving down as the song goes along.

### Benefits and drawbacks
Seeing words has always been great for things like spelling tests, and learning fact-based subjects. I don’t necessarily have a photographic memory, it’s more that I could remember what the sentence looked like, and each individual word in that sentence. When I hear new words, my brain tries to figure out what the word would look like, but I need to see the spelling of that word in order to internalise it.

The bad side of this, is that even when I’m already reading something externally, my brain still wants to repeat the words I’ve just seen on the paper, while my mind reads it out loud. This makes me a somewhat slower reader, unless I actively try not to read everything out loud in my head. This works okay for reading fiction, but not so much when I’m trying to remember what I’m reading.

### Language learning

This has been great for language learning, something I might be good at if I stuck to it long enough to become fluent. German for example, has been easier to pick up, as the spelling of words (and the sound of the words), bear a good resemblance to English and Afrikaans. This is because they are all Germanic languages. Therefore, while learning German, or when hearing words I know, the words will appear in my mind’s eye, too. Easy peasy.

This has been a bit different with some Polish words. The Polish language, being a Slavic language, makes use of a ton of consonants, so the spelling of words is way less intuitive for me. Shorter or easier words will appear the same way that the rest of the words in my brain do, but the longer words with more consonants get broken up into sounds or syllables.

Words like przepraszam (sorry/excuse me) or mężczyzna (man) will get fuzzy in my mind’s eye. It’s the equivalent of my mind writing ‘blah, blah, blah’, while I’ll switch over to what the word sounds like, since I can’t spell it.

It becomes pshu-pra-sham and mesh-chuz-na in my mind. Not in spelling, just in sound.

### Sound mapping
On the other hand, the mapping of specific letters in the Polish language, to the sounds they represent, has mostly been easy and intuitive for me. Some letters in the Polish alphabet, that are not in the English language, include:

Ł – Sounds like w in English

Ó – Sounds like the u in the word dude

Ź – Sounds like Zsh, as in the g in rouge (think pronunciation of Moulin rouge)

So what happens here, is you take a place name like Łódź, and along with the actual spelling of the name, I will also see the phonetic spelling that I hear when I see the word, which would be something more like ‘woodzsh’. 

And then my brain will break out in song, “Are we out of the Łódź, yet? Are we out of the Łódź?” (I’m not a Swiftie, but that is a great song).

### Unclear sounds
When I listen to music and can’t make out all the words, the words that my brain doesn’t recognise, kind of taper off in my mind’s eye. When the artist sings tu-du-dums, that’s what I see.

Tarra-rarra-rarra-dum.

And depending on the vibe of the song, the Tarra-rarra-rarra-dum might bounce around like the old Windows screensavers we used to have.

Obviously, watching English movies where the actors have really thick accents, is particularly weird. Because my auditory function struggles to pick up the words, I also don’t get any clear visuals of what the actors said. (Note: I always watch TV with subtitles on, it’s just way easier for me to process.)

### Do I ever see pictures?
A good example of times when I might want to use visuals, is in meditation. I don’t necessarily have aphantasia, as in, if I focus hard enough, I can conjure up visual imagery, but it is more difficult for me. I’d be more likely to be able to conjure up images of the words of the images that I want to see, than the actual images themselves.

A few of the people I asked about their perceptions, said they were more visual, and it seemed like the more visual people also dream way more than I do. Or more vividly. Maybe I just dream more in words and sounds. Who knows?

### Ticker-tape Synesthesia (TTS)
When I started writing this blog post, I wanted to write about my experience, without any outside influences. After Googling to find out if I have synesthesia, you’ll be glad to know that I do. It’s called Ticker-tape Synesthesia, and although it’s not ‘normal’, quite a few people have it.

Phew. Good to know.

According to studies, between 1.4% and 6.9% of the world’s population perceives the world like this, where the 1.4% of the population has Ticker-tape Synesthesia in its strictest definition. We (yes, I’m included in this part of the population), constantly, and automatically, see words rolling like subtitles, as we listen to people, hear songs and sounds, and even when our inner voices speak. The other 5.5% of people who have it, reportedly don’t have it all the time. 

### How do you ‘get’ Ticker-tape Synesthesia?

The Paris Brain Institute did a [study](https://parisbraininstitute.org/news/subtitled-world-uncovering-tickertape-synesthesia){:target="_blank"} with 26 participants that had Ticker-tape Synesthesia. They all filled in questionnaires, and according to those answers, it seems like 73% of the participants developed Ticker-tape when they acquired reading skills in childhood.

I can’t say for sure when I first became aware of my internal subtitles. My grandmother taught me to read at a young age, so by the time I went to school, I was already comfortable with reading the basic books that we had to read, and throughout my school years I had a love affair with books, language, and spelling tests. I just assumed that everyone else processed words and language the same way that I did.

It's been interesting to find out how people around me process the world. A great number of them are visual, with some people constantly counting things around them, and others that have weird inclinations like thinking about how to type certain words.

The bottom line is that we all have different and unique experiences when it comes to how we process the world. This has likely been shaped by our environments, the people around us, and our experiences (in childhood mostly).

This is so fascinating, isn’t it? Have you ever thought about the way you think?
